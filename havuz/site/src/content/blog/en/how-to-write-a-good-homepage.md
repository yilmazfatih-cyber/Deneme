Visitors do not read your homepage, they scan it. In the first few seconds they are looking for answers to three questions: **What is this?** **Is it for me?** **What should I do now?** The whole job of a homepage is to answer those questions before the visitor decides whether to scroll.

## Headline: say what people gain, not what you do

A headline such as "Innovative booking solutions" answers none of the three questions. Visitors care about how their own day will change, not about your product category.

| Weak headline                | Strong headline                       |
| ---------------------------- | ------------------------------------- |
| Innovative booking solutions | Fill the calendar, quiet the phone.   |
| Digital accounting platform  | Send the invoice, we handle the rest. |

A good headline makes a short, concrete promise that can be checked. Remove the adjectives; if the sentence still says something, you are on the right track.

## Pick one main action

If a homepage has five buttons, visitors press none of them. One main action (for example "Start free") and at most one secondary action (for example "How it works") is enough.

> [!TIP]
> Start button labels with a verb that describes what the visitor gets: "Start free", "Get a quote", "Book an appointment".

## Order sections by the visitor's questions

1. **Hero:** What do you do, and for whom?
2. **Features:** What exactly do I get?
3. **How it works:** Is it hard to start?
4. **Testimonials:** Are other people happy?
5. **Pricing:** How much is it?
6. **FAQ:** My last objections.
7. **Call to action:** Let me start now.

The homepage of this site follows exactly this order.

## A five-minute test before you publish

Show the page to someone who has never seen it for five seconds, then close it and ask: "What does this company do? Who is it for? What were you supposed to do?" If you get the right answer to all three, your homepage is doing its job.
